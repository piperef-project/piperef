Pipe basics

What is NPS, and why is the OD of a 6" pipe not 6 inches?

NPS (nominal pipe size) is a size name, not a measurement. From NPS 1/8 to NPS 12 the outside diameter is fixed by ASME B36.10M and differs from the nominal size (NPS 6 has an OD of 168.3 mm / 6.625 in). From NPS 14 upwards the OD equals the nominal size in inches. The OD stays the same for every schedule; only the wall, and so the bore, changes.

What is the difference between NPS and DN?

DN (diamètre nominal) is the metric size designation used in ISO and EN standards: NPS 2 = DN 50, NPS 6 = DN 150, NPS 24 = DN 600. For ASME pipe the DN is only a label; the actual OD is the same as the NPS pipe. EN/ISO pipe series can have different ODs for the same DN, so check which standard the project uses.

Link to this answer
What do pipe schedules mean?

A schedule (Sch 10, 40, 80, 160…) is a wall thickness series in ASME B36.10M. The higher the schedule, the thicker the wall for a given NPS. STD, XS and XXS are older weight classes; STD matches Sch 40 up to NPS 10 and XS matches Sch 80 up to NPS 8, but they differ above that, so never assume they are interchangeable on large sizes.

Link to this answer
What is the difference between Sch 40 and Sch 40S?

Sch 40S belongs to ASME B36.19M for stainless steel. For most sizes 40S equals Sch 40, but above NPS 10 they differ: 40S stays at 9.53 mm (STD) while Sch 40 gets heavier. Stainless lines usually use 5S or 10S where pressure allows, because stainless is expensive and resists corrosion without much allowance.

Seamless or welded pipe: how do I choose?

The piping class decides, but as a rule: seamless (e.g. ASTM A106 Gr B) for high pressure, high temperature, sour and cyclic service, and for small bore where welded pipe is uncommon; ERW or EFW welded pipe (e.g. A53, A671/A672) for larger sizes and utility or low-severity service. Welded pipe carries a weld joint quality factor E below 1.0 in B31.3 unless fully radiographed.

Link to this answer
What does "BE" and "PE" mean on pipe?

BE is bevelled ends, prepared for butt welding (typically 37.5° bevel for walls up to about 22 mm). PE is plain ends, cut square, used for socket weld or slip-on connections. TE or T&C is threaded ends (threaded and coupled). The end type appears in the MTO description and the piping class.

Link to this answer
What are SRL and DRL?

Pipe is supplied in random lengths: single random length (SRL, around 5–7 m, nominal 6 m) and double random length (DRL, around 11–13 m, nominal 12 m). The MTO converts total metres into lengths, adding a cutting and wastage allowance (often 3–10%).

Link to this answer
What is corrosion allowance and how is it used?

Corrosion allowance is extra wall added to the pressure design thickness to cover metal loss over the design life, typically 1.5–3 mm for carbon steel and 0 for stainless in clean service. It is set in the piping class from the process and materials study. It is added before mill tolerance when checking the minimum wall.

Link to this answer
Why is mill tolerance 12.5%?

Seamless pipe made to ASTM A106 / A530 may have a wall up to 12.5% thinner than nominal at any point. So the nominal wall must be at least the required thickness divided by 0.875. Plate-formed welded pipe often has a smaller under-tolerance (e.g. 0.3 mm), so check the material specification.

Link to this answer
Which pipe materials are common in oil and gas?

Carbon steel (A106 B, A333 Gr 6 for low temperature) for most process and utility service; low-alloy Cr-Mo (A335 P11, P22) for high temperature; stainless 304L/316L (A312) for corrosive and cryogenic service; duplex and super duplex for seawater and chlorides; nickel alloys for severe corrosion; and non-metallics (GRE/GRP, HDPE) for firewater, cooling water and drains. The piping class lists exactly which grades are allowed.

Fittings

What is the difference between LR and SR elbows?

A long radius (LR) elbow has a centreline radius of 1.5 × NPS; a short radius (SR) elbow has 1.0 × NPS. LR is standard because it has lower pressure drop and is easier to pig and inspect. SR is used only where space forces it, and some services (slurry, erosive, pigged lines) forbid it.

Link to this answer
When do you use a pipe bend instead of an elbow?

Bends (3D or 5D radius, made by induction bending) are used for pigged lines, erosive or two-phase flow, and long-distance pipelines, where a smooth large radius is needed. They save welds but need straight tangents at each end and cost more per piece. Bends are thinned on the outer side (extrados), so the starting wall must allow for thinning.

Link to this answer
What is the difference between a concentric and an eccentric reducer?

A concentric reducer keeps the centreline; an eccentric reducer keeps one side flat. On pump suctions, use an eccentric reducer flat side up (FOT) so no vapour pocket forms. On pipe racks and sleepers, use flat side down (FOB) so the bottom of pipe stays level on the supports.

Link to this answer
Tee or branch connection: which do I use?

The piping class has a branch table that tells you, for each header and branch size, whether to use an equal or reducing tee, a weldolet/sockolet, a stub-in with or without reinforcement, or a half-coupling. Tees are preferred for large branches; olets for small branches off large headers. Always follow the branch table rather than habit.

Link to this answer
What are weldolet, sockolet and threadolet?

They are integrally reinforced branch outlet fittings (MSS SP-97) welded onto a header. Weldolet gives a butt-weld branch, sockolet a socket-weld branch and threadolet a threaded branch. Sweepolet and latrolet (45°) are variants. They are the standard way to take small branches, instrument connections, vents and drains off larger lines.

Link to this answer
Socket weld or butt weld for small bore?

Socket weld (ASME B16.11) is common up to NPS 1½ or 2 because it is quick to fit up. It leaves a crevice and a 1.6 mm expansion gap at the bottom of the socket, so many specifications forbid it in corrosive, cyclic, sour or high-vibration service, and require butt weld instead. Threaded joints are limited further, usually to utilities and instrument air.

Link to this answer
Why must you leave a gap in a socket weld?

Before welding, the pipe is pulled back about 1.6 mm (1/16 in) from the socket bottom. Without the gap, weld shrinkage and thermal expansion load the root and the joint can crack. Inspectors often check it with a gap ring or by marking the pipe before fit-up.

Link to this answer
What is a swage (swage nipple)?

A swage nipple is a forged reducer for small bore, often joining socket weld or threaded ends of different sizes (MSS SP-95). It is used on vents, drains and instrument connections where a butt-weld reducer would be too small. It can be concentric or eccentric and comes with different end combinations, e.g. large end plain, small end threaded (LBE × TSE).

Link to this answer
What is a stub end and when is it used?

A stub end is a short pipe piece with a flared lap, used with a lap-joint (loose) flange. The stub end is welded to the pipe in the expensive material while the flange can be cheaper carbon steel. It also makes bolt holes easy to align. Common on stainless, alloy and lined pipe.

Link to this answer
What is the take-off of a fitting?

Take-off is the length a fitting adds to the pipe run, measured from its centre (or end) to the welding end. For a 90° LR elbow it equals the radius, 1.5 × NPS; for a 45° LR elbow it is R × tan 22.5°. Pipe cut lengths on isometrics are dimensions minus take-offs minus weld gaps.

Flanges, gaskets and bolting

What do flange classes 150, 300, 600 mean?

They are pressure–temperature rating designations in ASME B16.5 (and B16.47). The allowable pressure depends on the class, the material group and the temperature; for carbon steel at ambient, Class 150 is about 19.6 bar and Class 300 about 51.1 bar, falling as temperature rises. The class is not a pressure in psi.

Link to this answer
Weld neck, slip-on, socket weld, lap joint: which flange where?

Weld neck (WN) is the default for process lines: full-penetration butt weld, good for high pressure, temperature and cycling. Slip-on (SO) is cheaper and used on low-severity utility service. Socket weld (SW) is for small bore. Lap joint with stub end is for expensive alloys and easy bolt alignment. Blind flanges close ends and nozzles.

Link to this answer
What is the difference between RF, FF and RTJ facing?

Raised face (RF) is the standard; the gasket sits on a raised ring (1.6 mm for Class 150/300, 6.4 mm for Class 400 and up). Flat face (FF) is used against cast iron or non-metallic flanges so bolting does not crack them. Ring type joint (RTJ) uses a metal ring in a groove, for high pressure and temperature, usually Class 900 and above.

Link to this answer
When do you use B16.47 Series A and Series B?

ASME B16.47 covers flanges NPS 26 to 60. Series A (formerly MSS SP-44) are heavier with bigger bolts; Series B (formerly API 605) are lighter and more compact. They are not interchangeable, so the series must be stated on the P&ID data, the piping class and the vendor's equipment flanges.

Link to this answer
Which gasket is used where?

Spiral wound gaskets (ASME B16.20) with graphite filler and inner and outer rings are standard for process lines from Class 150 upward. Compressed non-asbestos sheet (ASME B16.21) is used for utilities and Class 150 water. Kammprofile gaskets suit heat exchangers and high-cycle joints. Ring joints are used with RTJ flanges. The piping class names the gasket per rating and service.

Link to this answer
Why should a gasket never be reused?

A gasket seals by deforming permanently into the flange surface. Once compressed it cannot recover enough to seal again, and spiral wound windings may have buckled. Reusing gaskets is one of the most common causes of leaks at start-up. Always fit a new, correctly rated gasket.

Link to this answer
What bolting material is standard?

ASTM A193 B7 studs with A194 2H heavy hex nuts for carbon steel service from about −29 °C to 400 °C+. A320 L7 with A194 Gr 7 for low temperature. A193 B8M / A194 8M for stainless service. Coated bolting (e.g. PTFE or zinc-nickel) is common offshore and in corrosive atmospheres.

Link to this answer
How is stud bolt length worked out?

Length = 2 × flange thickness + gasket + 2 nut heights + a projection of about 2–3 threads at each end, with raised face height added for Class 400 and up, rounded up to the next standard length. See the Bolt length calculator for the build-up and a worked example.

Link to this answer
What is flange management or bolt torquing per PCC-1?

ASME PCC-1 gives guidelines for assembling bolted flanged joints: clean and inspect faces, align, lubricate, tighten in a cross pattern in several passes (for example 30%, 70%, 100% of target), then check clockwise. Critical joints are tagged and their torque recorded. See the Flange joint page.

Link to this answer
What is a spectacle blind and when is it used?

A spectacle (figure-8) blind is a solid disc and a ring joined by a web, installed between flanges for positive isolation. One side blocks flow, the other lets it through, and the visible shape shows which position it is in. Large sizes use separate spades and spacers because a spectacle would be too heavy. ASME B16.48 gives dimensions.

Valves

Gate, globe, ball, butterfly: what is each used for?

Gate valves are for on/off isolation with low pressure drop. Globe valves throttle and regulate flow. Ball valves give quick quarter-turn on/off, tight shutoff and are piggable when full-bore. Butterfly valves are compact and light for large diameters at low to moderate pressure. Check valves stop reverse flow.

Link to this answer
Why is a globe valve installed with flow under the seat?

With flow entering under the disc, the packing is not exposed to upstream pressure when the valve is closed, and the valve opens more easily against pressure. Flow over the seat is used on some high-pressure or fail-close applications to help close the valve. The flow arrow cast on the body shows the intended direction.

Link to this answer
Where should valves be located for operation?

Operate-and-maintain valves should be reachable from grade or a platform. A common practice: handwheels between about 0.75 m and 1.5 m above floor are ideal, up to about 2 m acceptable; higher valves need chain wheels or extension spindles, or a platform. Valves operated in an emergency need clear, quick access. See the Valve access page.

Link to this answer
Can a check valve be installed in a vertical line?

Swing checks work in vertical lines only with flow upwards, and only if the design allows it. Lift checks may need horizontal installation depending on type. Dual-plate wafer checks work in any orientation with upward or horizontal flow. Always confirm with the valve datasheet; never install a check with flow downwards.

Link to this answer
What is a double block and bleed?

Two isolation valves in series with a bleed valve between them, so the cavity can be vented and the isolation proven. It is used for maintenance isolation on hazardous service and on instrument connections. It can be two separate valves or a single body DBB valve.

Link to this answer
What is the difference between full-bore and reduced-bore ball valves?

A full-bore valve has a bore equal to the pipe ID, so pigs can pass and pressure drop is minimal. A reduced-bore valve has a bore about one size smaller; it is cheaper and lighter and acceptable in most process service. Pigged lines and some relief paths require full bore.

Link to this answer
Why do control valves have bypasses and block valves?

Block valves on both sides let the control valve be removed while the plant runs; the bypass globe valve lets operators control flow by hand meanwhile. Many modern designs omit bypasses on reliable or critical-service valves; the P&ID shows which apply. Layout must allow removal of the valve and actuator.

Link to this answer
What are valve end connections?

Flanged (RF or RTJ) for most process valves above NPS 1½; butt-weld ends for high pressure and temperature, critical tight systems and large forged valves; socket weld or threaded for small bore forged valves (Class 800 to API 602). The piping class valve table lists the allowed end types per size and rating.

Link to this answer
What does valve "trim" mean?

Trim is the set of internal wetted parts that seal and wear: seat, disc or wedge, stem and back seat. API 600 lists trim numbers, such as trim 8 (13Cr with hardfaced seats). The body may be carbon steel while the trim is stainless or stellited to resist wear and corrosion.

Link to this answer
What are relief valve inlet and outlet piping rules?

Inlet piping should be short and self-draining, sized so pressure loss is within about 3% of set pressure (API 520 Part II). Outlet piping to a closed flare header should slope back to the header with no pockets and enter from the top. Outlet reaction forces need supports. Isolation valves under a PSV must be locked or car-sealed open.

Pipe supports

What is the difference between a rest, guide, line stop and anchor?

A rest (resting support) carries the weight only. A guide also stops sideways movement but lets the pipe slide axially. A line stop stops axial movement in one or both directions. An anchor fixes the pipe in all directions. Stress analysis decides which type goes where.

Link to this answer
What is a pipe shoe for?

A shoe is a steel piece welded or clamped under the pipe so it rests on the steel at a height that clears the insulation. It also protects the pipe wall from wear when it slides. Shoe height is chosen so the insulation clears the beam; typical shoe heights are 100, 150 and 200 mm.

Link to this answer
How far apart can supports be?

It depends on size, wall, contents, insulation and allowable sag. A widely used table is ASME B31.1 Table 121.5 for standard-wall steel pipe; for example, NPS 6 water service about 5.2 m. Spans are reduced near valves, flanges and changes of direction. See the Support spans page.

Link to this answer
What is a dummy leg?

A dummy leg is a short length of pipe welded to an elbow, extending down to a support, so a line that turns down can be supported at the elbow. It must be checked for local stress on the elbow and needs a vent or weep hole. Dummy legs on high-temperature or thin-wall lines need special attention.

Link to this answer
When are spring supports used?

Spring hangers or supports carry the weight of lines that move vertically with temperature, where a rigid support would lift off or overload. Variable springs allow some load change; constant springs keep the load almost constant for large movements. Stress analysis selects the spring type and the installed and operating loads.

Link to this answer
What is a trunnion?

A trunnion is a pipe or plate section welded to an elbow or straight pipe to transfer load to a support, often used on large lines. Like dummy legs, trunnions need local stress checks, and the support steel must allow for the pipe's thermal movement.

Link to this answer
Why are PTFE or slide plates used on supports?

Sliding plates with low friction (PTFE, graphite or stainless-on-PTFE) reduce the friction force when hot lines move on their supports. That lowers loads on the steel and anchors. They are used where friction would otherwise govern the support or nozzle loads.

Link to this answer
What should be checked when routing near supports?

Keep welds, flanges and branches clear of the support (often at least 50 mm from weld to shoe), avoid supports on valves and instruments, keep access for maintenance, and confirm the support location on steel that exists or is planned. Coordinate new support loads with civil and structural.

Link to this answer
What is a clamp support vs welded support?

Clamped supports avoid welding on the pipe, which is required for some materials (stainless, galvanized, lined or post-weld heat-treated pipe) and for existing live lines. Welded supports are stronger and cheaper. The support standard shows which types are allowed in which piping classes.

Link to this answer
Who decides support types and locations?

The piping designer proposes supports to suit the layout and the support standard. The stress engineer confirms or changes them for critical lines (high temperature, large bore, rotating equipment connections, relief lines). Civil and structural design the steel for the resulting loads.

Piping layout

What inputs does a piping layout designer need?

Plot plan, P&IDs, line list, equipment layout and vendor drawings, piping material specification, design standards, process requirements (slopes, straight runs, no pockets), stress critical line list, structural and civil models, and the client's operation and maintenance philosophy.

Link to this answer
What are the main layout principles?

Route lines in parallel groups along rack or sleeper corridors; keep them in orthogonal directions; group lines at standard elevations (one elevation for north-south, another for east-west); avoid pockets in vapour lines and dead legs; keep access ways clear; allow flexibility for thermal growth; and minimise length and fittings without blocking maintenance.

Link to this answer
What is a dead leg and why avoid it?

A dead leg is a section of pipe with no flow, such as a closed branch. Fluid stagnates there, causing corrosion, freezing, solids build-up or bacterial growth. Keep dead legs short (typical rules limit them to a few pipe diameters) or route branches from the top or side as specified.

Link to this answer
Why is pump suction piping so critical?

Pumps need steady, low-loss suction flow without vapour. Keep suction lines short and direct, slope continuously towards the pump without high points, use eccentric reducers flat side up, and provide straight pipe before the pump (often at least 5 × D) or as the vendor requires. Elbows directly at the pump inlet in the wrong plane can cause cavitation.

Link to this answer
What clearances are typical for access ways?

Typical practice: main access ways around 1.2 m wide minimum, secondary walkways 0.8 m, 2.1 m headroom over walkways, and larger clear areas for maintenance vehicles and cranes. Escape routes must stay clear. Project standards set the exact values; see the Access & maintenance page.

Link to this answer
How do you plan for maintenance?

Show removal spaces (tube bundle pulling, pump and motor removal, control valve removal) as reserved volumes in the 3D model. Provide lifting points, davits or monorails where needed. Flanged spools allow removal of equipment. Drop zones must be kept free of piping.

Link to this answer
How do you handle lines that need slope?

Gravity lines (drains, flare headers, sulphur and some two-phase lines) must slope continuously in the direction of flow without pockets. Set the slope on the P&ID or line list (e.g. 1:200 for flare headers). Check every support elevation along the run and allow for structure deflection.

Link to this answer
What is jacketed or traced piping?

Jacketed piping has an outer pipe around the process pipe, with steam or hot oil in the annulus to keep the fluid hot, used for sulphur, bitumen and high-melting products. Traced piping has small tubes (steam or electric heat tracing) clamped along the line. Both need careful layout for jumpers, supports, expansion and drainage.

Link to this answer
How are lines to and from tanks routed?

Tank nozzles move with tank settlement and bulging, so the first support away from the nozzle must allow movement, and flexibility is needed in the piping. Lines inside a bund are kept low; bund penetrations need sleeves; and fire safety rules separate tanks from other equipment.

Link to this answer
How is safety built into layout?

Keep hazardous equipment apart as the spacing standard requires, place emergency isolation valves at the battery limit and outside fire zones, route relief lines to flare without pockets, provide two escape routes from platforms, and keep fire water, safety showers and monitors accessible.

Pipe racks

How are lines arranged on a pipe rack?

Larger and heavier lines go near the columns, smaller lines in the middle. Process lines usually go on the lower tier, utilities above, cable trays on top. Flare and relief headers go on the top pipe tier at the outside so they can slope. Hot lines with expansion loops go at the outside edge so the loops can turn up or out. Future space is left in the middle of each tier.

Link to this answer
How much future space is normal?

Many projects reserve 20–25% of the rack width for future lines, sometimes up to 30% on brownfield or phased projects. The client's design basis states the value.

Link to this answer
How far apart are rack tiers?

Typically 1.5 to 2 m between tiers, enough for the largest line plus branch connections turning up or down. Rack height at the lowest tier must give clearance for roads (often 5–7 m) and for walkways under the rack.

Link to this answer
How wide is a pipe rack?

It depends on the number and size of lines. Common widths are 6, 8 or 10 m between column centres, in modules that suit the steel and foundations. Very wide racks are split into more tiers or a double rack. See the Pipe rack calculator for a first estimate.

Link to this answer
Why are expansion loops placed in the middle between anchors?

So that each side grows the same amount towards the loop and the loop legs share the displacement. Loops on racks are usually nested, with the largest or hottest line on the outside, and turn up over the other lines or out past the rack edge.

Link to this answer
What are the typical rack spacings between lines?

Centre-to-centre is based on the larger flange OD of one line plus the OD and insulation of the next, plus a clearance (often 25 mm), with flanges staggered. See the Pipe spacing page.

Link to this answer
What is the difference between a rack and a sleeper?

A pipe rack is elevated steel or concrete structure. Sleepers are low concrete or steel beams at grade, typically in offsites and tank farms, where lines run near the ground. Sleepers are cheaper but block access, so crossings need pipe bridges or stiles.

Link to this answer
What loads does piping put on a rack?

Vertical weight (pipe, contents, insulation, test water for the largest lines), friction from thermal movement, anchor and guide loads from stress analysis, wind and seismic. Civil and structural design the rack from loading data issued by piping and stress.

Link to this answer
Do cable trays share the rack?

Often yes: electrical and instrument cable trays go on a dedicated top tier or at one side, separated from hot lines. Electrical and instrument trays are usually separated from each other. Fire-proofing may be needed on the rack structure and on critical cables.

Link to this answer
What happens at rack crossings and branches?

Lines leave the rack by turning up or down, then running to equipment. Changes of elevation keep north–south and east–west lines on separate elevations so they cross without clashes. Branches on the rack should be from the top for gas and from the bottom for liquid where process allows.

Equipment piping

How should piping connect to pumps?

Keep suction short with no pockets, use eccentric reducers flat side up, provide strainers (temporary for start-up, permanent where specified), support the pipe so the pump nozzles carry no weight, and check nozzle loads against API 610 or the vendor's limits. Leave space for pump and motor removal.

Link to this answer
What is important for heat exchanger piping?

Leave the tube bundle pulling area clear, use removable spools and flanges for channel covers, keep piping away from the channel end, arrange cooling water so the exchanger stays full, and provide vents and drains on each side. Stacked exchangers need careful alignment of nozzles.

Link to this answer
How are columns (towers) piped?

Lines run vertically down the column on the piping side, supported and guided from the shell with brackets. Platforms and ladders are on the access side. Manways, relief valves and instruments need platform access. Reflux and overhead lines need slope and flexibility for thermal growth of the tall shell.

Link to this answer
What are nozzle load limits?

Equipment nozzles can take only limited forces and moments from piping. Pumps follow API 610, compressors API 617/NEMA SM-23, and vessels and exchangers are checked by WRC 107/537 or the vendor's allowables. Stress analysis confirms the piping keeps within them; supports and flexibility are added if not.

Link to this answer
Why is compressor piping special?

Compressor connections have tight nozzle load limits and pulsation or vibration issues (especially reciprocating compressors). Piping needs rigid, often independent supports, pulsation studies, flexibility for thermal growth, and space for maintenance and suction strainers.

Link to this answer
How is piping arranged around air coolers?

Inlet headers feed the bundle from the top; outlet lines leave from the bottom. Multiple bays need balanced (symmetrical) piping so flow splits evenly. Thermal growth of the headers needs flexibility. Access for fan maintenance under the cooler must stay clear.

Link to this answer
What about reactor and fired heater piping?

These run at high temperature and need detailed stress analysis, spring supports, and careful material selection (often Cr-Mo alloys). Heater coils move with temperature, so connected piping must be flexible. Catalyst loading and unloading space must be kept free.

Link to this answer
What is a strainer and where is it placed?

A strainer protects equipment from debris. Temporary (cone or basket) strainers sit in pump suctions during commissioning; permanent Y, basket or duplex strainers protect control valves, meters and equipment in service. Allow space for removal and a spool to remove temporary strainers.

Link to this answer
How do you route relief valve discharge from equipment?

PSVs on vessels are mounted at the top on short inlet piping. Discharge goes to flare without pockets, entering the header from the top, or to atmosphere at a safe location if allowed. Supports must take the reaction force when the valve lifts.

Link to this answer
What drawings do you need from equipment vendors?

General arrangement with nozzle orientation, size, rating, facing and elevations; nozzle load limits; foundation and baseplate details; maintenance and removal spaces; and instrument and utility connection lists. Piping layout is held until certified vendor data is received for critical items.

3D modelling (S3D, E3D)

What does a 3D piping model contain?

Equipment, structures, piping with components from the specification, supports, cable trays, instruments, valves with operators, access and maintenance volumes, and reserved spaces. Each item carries attributes (line number, spec, size, insulation, tracing, test pressure) used to produce isometrics, MTOs and reports.

Link to this answer
What is a piping specification in the 3D tool?

The catalogue and specification database in S3D, E3D or PDMS that defines which components are allowed in each piping class, by size: pipe, fittings, flanges, gaskets, bolts, valves and branch rules. The modeller selects a spec and the tool places only compliant components. Wrong spec data causes wrong MTOs, so spec changes are controlled.

Link to this answer
What are model reviews (30%, 60%, 90%)?

Formal reviews with the client and disciplines at stages of model completion. 30% confirms layout concept, equipment positions, main routing and access. 60% checks most routing, supports and operability. 90% checks completeness, clashes, maintenance and safety before isometrics are issued for construction. Comments are tracked to closure.

Link to this answer
How are clashes managed?

The tool runs clash checks between disciplines; hard clashes (physical overlap) and soft clashes (clearance or insulation) are listed and assigned to the responsible discipline. Clash status is reported weekly and must be zero (or approved) before issuing for construction.

Link to this answer
What is the difference between S3D and E3D?

Smart 3D (Hexagon) and AVEVA E3D (formerly PDMS) are both plant design systems with specification-driven piping, clash checking, isometric extraction and reports. They differ in database structure, catalogue management, customisation and drawing tools. Many EPC companies standardise on one per project, set by the client.

Link to this answer
How are isometrics extracted from the model?

The tool exports each line (or pipeline) to an isometric generator (e.g. Isogen) with settings for symbols, dimensions, weld numbers, cut lists and BOM. Extracted isos are checked against the P&ID, line list and model before issue. Changes are then made in the model, not on the drawing.

Link to this answer
What is a PCF file?

A Piping Component File is a text export of a line's components, coordinates and attributes, used by Isogen and many tools (stress import, revision compare, fabrication software). The Iso compare tool can compare two PCF files to list component changes between revisions.

Link to this answer
How do you keep model attributes correct?

Use a line list as the master and update the model from it; run attribute and integrity checks (missing specs, wrong insulation, unconnected ends); and use project reports to compare the model with P&IDs. Bad attributes produce wrong isos and MTOs, so data checks are as important as clash checks.

Link to this answer
How do supports get modelled?

Supports are placed from a catalogue of standard types with parameters (shoe height, guide type, steel member). Each carries a tag and links to its support detail drawing and BOM. Stress-critical supports are placed or confirmed by the stress engineer.

Link to this answer
What is "as-built" modelling?

At the end of construction, the model and isometrics are updated to match what was actually built (field changes, routing changes, support moves). Brownfield work often starts with laser scans of the plant, from which existing piping is modelled or used as a reference for new tie-ins.

Isometrics and MTO

What information is on an isometric?

Line number, size, spec, insulation, tracing, design and test conditions, P&ID reference, the 3D route with dimensions and coordinates, fittings, flanges, valves, supports, welds (shop and field), cut lengths, and a bill of materials. It is the drawing used to fabricate and erect the line.

Link to this answer
What is the difference between shop and field welds?

Shop welds are made in the fabrication shop where conditions are controlled; field welds are made on site during erection. Field welds are minimised because they cost more and are harder to inspect. Isos mark field fit-up welds (FFW) with extra length so the welder can cut to fit.

Link to this answer
What is a spool?

A spool is a section of a line fabricated in the shop, sized for transport and erection, between field welds or flanges. Spools are numbered on the isometric and tracked through fabrication, painting, testing and erection.

Link to this answer
What does an iso checker look for?

Agreement with P&ID (all valves, instruments, reducers, vents and drains), correct spec and branch connections, dimensions and coordinates that close, supports at the stress-defined locations, correct insulation and test data, and a BOM that matches the model. Common errors are missing vents and drains, wrong reducer orientation and wrong spec breaks.

Link to this answer
What is an MTO?

A Material Take-Off is the list of quantities of all piping materials (pipe metres, fittings, flanges, valves, gaskets, bolts, supports) by size, rating and material. Early MTOs from P&IDs and the model are used for procurement of long-lead items; final MTOs come from issued isometrics. See the MTO calculator.

Link to this answer
Why are MTO allowances added?

Pipe needs cutting and wastage allowance; small items get lost on site; bolts and gaskets are needed for commissioning, testing and later breaks. Typical surplus is 3–10% on pipe, 5–10% on small fittings and 10–20% on gaskets and bolts, as set by the project.

Link to this answer
What is a spec break?

The point where one piping class changes to another, e.g. at a control valve or block valve where design conditions change. It is shown on the P&ID and on the isometric, and the components on each side must belong to the correct class.

Link to this answer
What are weld numbers used for?

Each weld is numbered on the isometric so its welder, procedure, NDE results and PWHT can be traced in the weld map and QA records. Weld numbering is required for inspection and turnover dossiers.

Link to this answer
How are revisions controlled on isometrics?

Each change gets a new revision, clouded and noted with a reason. Superseded issues are withdrawn from site. Comparing revisions (manually or with tools like Iso compare) shows exactly what changed so fabrication and erection can act on it.

Link to this answer
What is a line list?

A table of every line with its number, size, from–to, piping class, fluid, design and operating pressure and temperature, test pressure and medium, insulation, tracing, NDE category, PWHT and stress criticality. It is the master data source for model attributes and isometric title blocks.

Stress analysis for designers

Which lines need formal stress analysis?

Projects publish stress criticality criteria. Typical triggers: high temperature (e.g. above about 150–200 °C depending on size), large diameter, connections to rotating equipment, relief lines, lines with large thermal movement, cyclic or vibrating service, and non-metallic or lined pipe. Others are checked by visual or simplified methods.

Link to this answer
What is the difference between sustained, thermal and occasional stress?

Sustained stress comes from weight and pressure, which are always present. Thermal (expansion) stress comes from restrained thermal growth and varies with temperature cycles. Occasional stress comes from short events: wind, earthquake, relief valve reaction, slug or water hammer. B31.3 limits each separately.

Link to this answer
Why do lines need flexibility?

When a hot line grows between two fixed points, the pipe must bend somewhere to absorb the movement. Without enough flexibility (offsets, loops, bends), stresses and loads on equipment become too high. Layout provides flexibility by routing; stress analysis confirms it.

Link to this answer
What does the stress engineer need from the designer?

A clean, attributed model or isometrics with the route, supports, equipment nozzles and their movements, line list data (temperatures, pressures, materials, insulation), and any special requirements. Early sketches of critical lines save rework later.

Link to this answer
What is cold spring?

Cold spring (cold pull) means fabricating a line short so it is pulled into place at installation, reducing the hot reaction. B31.3 does not credit it in the stress range calculation, and it is hard to control in the field, so it is used rarely and documented carefully.

Link to this answer
Why are nozzle loads a common problem?

Pumps and compressors have low allowable loads; a short, stiff line between equipment and the rack can easily exceed them. Fixes are more flexibility (extra loop or offset), better support arrangement, or springs. Early coordination with stress avoids late rerouting.

Link to this answer
What is the guided cantilever method?

A quick hand method that treats each leg of a loop as a cantilever guided at its end, giving the leg length needed to absorb a displacement: L = √(3 E D Δ / S_A). It is used for early sizing; see the Expansion loop page.

Link to this answer
What causes vibration in piping?

Pulsation from reciprocating machines, flow-induced turbulence, two-phase slug flow, cavitation and flashing at valves, and poorly supported small-bore branches. Small-bore connections near vibrating sources are a leading cause of fatigue leaks; they are braced or kept short.

Link to this answer
Why are relief valve outlets checked for reaction forces?

When a PSV lifts, the discharging fluid creates a large thrust on the outlet. Without proper supports, the valve and inlet piping can be overstressed or break. Stress and support design take the reaction from API 520 calculations.

Link to this answer
What software is used for stress analysis?

CAESAR II and AutoPIPE are the most common; ROHR2 and others are also used. They model the pipe as beam elements with supports and loads and check stresses against B31.3 or other codes, plus nozzle loads and displacements.

Codes and standards

What is ASME B31.3?

The Process Piping code, used for refineries, chemical and petrochemical plants, terminals and similar facilities. It covers materials, design, fabrication, examination and testing. Power plants use B31.1; pipelines use B31.4 (liquids) and B31.8 (gas).

Link to this answer
What is the difference between ASME B31.1 and B31.3?

B31.1 covers power piping (boiler external piping, steam systems in power plants) and is generally more conservative in allowable stresses and some details. B31.3 covers process plants. The project scope, the client and local regulation decide which applies.

Link to this answer
Which standards cover dimensions of components?

ASME B36.10M / B36.19M (pipe), B16.9 (butt-weld fittings), B16.11 (forged socket weld and threaded fittings), B16.5 and B16.47 (flanges), B16.20 and B16.21 (gaskets), B16.10 (valve face-to-face), B16.48 (line blinds), MSS SP-97 (branch outlets).

Link to this answer
Which standards cover valves?

API 600 (steel gate valves), API 602 (small forged valves), API 608 (ball), API 609 (butterfly), API 594 (check), API 6D (pipeline valves), BS 1873 / API 623 (globe), and API 598 (inspection and testing).

Link to this answer
What does a piping material specification (PMS) contain?

For each piping class: design limits, corrosion allowance, materials, and the allowed components by size range with their standards and end types, plus branch tables and notes. The PMS is the single reference for what can be used in each line.

Link to this answer
What is NACE MR0175 / ISO 15156?

A standard for materials in sour (H₂S-containing) service, limiting hardness and specifying acceptable alloys to prevent sulphide stress cracking. Lines in sour service have special piping classes and inspection requirements.

Link to this answer
What is PWHT and when is it required?

Post-weld heat treatment relieves weld residual stresses and tempers hard zones. B31.3 requires it for thicker walls and certain alloys (e.g. many Cr-Mo grades), and process conditions (caustic, amine, sour, hydrogen service) often require it regardless of thickness. The line list and PMS state it.

Link to this answer
What are the main NDE methods?

Visual testing (VT), radiography (RT) or ultrasonic testing (UT) for weld volume, magnetic particle (MT) and dye penetrant (PT) for surface cracks, and positive material identification (PMI) for alloys. B31.3 sets minimum examination by fluid service category; projects often require more.

Link to this answer
What are B31.3 fluid service categories?

Normal fluid service; Category D (non-flammable, non-toxic, low pressure and temperature); Category M (very toxic, where a small leak is dangerous); High Pressure; and severe cyclic conditions. The category affects design, examination and testing requirements.

Link to this answer
Why must the code edition be checked?

Codes are revised regularly; allowable stresses, tables and rules change between editions. The contract names the edition that applies. Always use that edition, not simply the newest one or a value remembered from another project.

How EPC projects work

What are the main phases of a plant project?

Feasibility, concept, FEED (front-end engineering design), then EPC: detailed engineering, procurement and construction, followed by pre-commissioning, commissioning, start-up and handover. Piping work peaks in detailed engineering and construction.

Link to this answer
What does the piping discipline deliver?

Plot plans and equipment layouts, piping material specifications, line lists, the 3D model, piping GA and key plans, isometrics, support drawings and BOM, MTOs, stress reports, specialty item datasheets, tie-in lists and as-built documentation.

Link to this answer
Who does what in a piping team?

The lead piping engineer manages scope, schedule and interfaces; layout designers route and model; material engineers own the PMS, MTO and valve datasheets; stress engineers do flexibility analysis and support design for critical lines; support designers detail supports; checkers review isos and drawings.

Link to this answer
How does piping interface with other disciplines?

Process (P&IDs, line data), mechanical (equipment data and nozzle loads), civil and structural (racks, foundations, platforms), instrumentation (instrument locations, control valves), electrical (trays, cable routes), and HSE (escape, spacing, fire protection). Interface queries and holds are tracked to closure.

Link to this answer
What is a "hold" on a drawing?

A hold marks information not yet confirmed, such as an awaited vendor nozzle orientation. Held items cannot be fabricated. Holds are listed, tracked and released when data arrives.

Link to this answer
Why does vendor data matter so much?

Piping connects to equipment; until the vendor certifies nozzle sizes, positions and loads, routing near the equipment is provisional. Late vendor data is one of the biggest causes of piping rework, so critical vendor drawings are chased early.

Link to this answer
What is a technical query (TQ)?

A formal question raised when information is missing, unclear or conflicting, e.g. between P&ID and line list. It gets a reference number, an owner and a response date, so decisions are recorded and traceable.

Link to this answer
What happens during construction support?

Site queries come back to engineering (site technical queries or field change requests), isos are revised for field changes, and engineering reviews deviations. Engineers may be posted on site to resolve problems quickly.

Link to this answer
What is pre-commissioning and commissioning?

Pre-commissioning includes flushing, blowing, pressure testing, reinstatement and dry-out of systems. Commissioning brings systems into service with process fluids under controlled procedures. Piping must be complete, tested and punch-list items closed before handover.

Link to this answer
What is a punch list?

A list of outstanding items found during walkdowns before handover: Category A items must be fixed before commissioning; Category B items can be finished later. Typical piping punch items include missing supports, gaskets, bolts, insulation, tags or test blinds not removed.

Fabrication, testing and construction

What is a hydrostatic test?

The line is filled with water and pressurised to the test pressure (for B31.3, normally 1.5 × design pressure, adjusted for temperature), held while all joints are inspected for leaks, then drained. Test packs group lines tested together and list test limits, blinds and vents.

Link to this answer
When is a pneumatic test used instead?

When water cannot be used (cryogenic, process contamination, weight limits on supports or structures). B31.3 pneumatic test pressure is normally 1.1 × design pressure. It stores far more energy than a hydrotest, so it needs a strict safety procedure, exclusion zones and engineering approval.

Link to this answer
What is a test pack?

A document package for testing a group of lines: marked-up isos and P&IDs, test limits, test pressure and medium, blinds and spades list, vent and drain points, gauge calibration and sign-offs. After testing it becomes part of the handover records.

Link to this answer
Why are temporary supports needed during hydrotest?

Water is much heavier than gas. Lines designed for gas or vapour may need temporary supports, and springs must be locked, so the extra weight does not overload the pipe or structure.

Link to this answer
What are common fabrication defects?

Misalignment (hi-lo), lack of fusion or penetration, porosity, undercut, wrong root gap, wrong material, missing PWHT, and dimensional errors. Inspection and NDE catch them; correct weld procedures and qualified welders prevent them.

Link to this answer
What is reinstatement after testing?

After testing, temporary blinds, spades and test spools are removed, permanent gaskets and valves installed, instruments refitted and the line returned to its design configuration. A missed blind or wrong gasket after reinstatement is a classic start-up problem.

Link to this answer
What is flushing and blowing?

Flushing with water or another fluid, and blowing with air or steam, removes debris, weld spatter and scale before equipment is connected. Pump suctions get temporary strainers. Cleanliness levels are verified, e.g. with target plates for steam lines.

Link to this answer
Why is PMI done on alloy piping?

Positive material identification checks that the alloy components and welds are the specified material. Mix-ups between carbon steel and alloy fittings can cause rapid failure in high-temperature or corrosive service, so PMI is common on alloy systems.

Link to this answer
What should be checked during piping erection?

Correct spool and orientation, gaskets and bolts per spec, flange alignment without forcing, supports installed as designed, spring locks in place, slopes achieved, valves with correct flow direction, and clearance for insulation and access.

Link to this answer
What causes most piping rework on site?

Late or changed vendor data, model errors not caught by clash checks, wrong or missing supports, field routing around unmodelled items (cables, small bore, existing plant), material shortages, and misread isometrics. Good model reviews and data checks prevent much of it.

Safety in piping design

How does piping design affect plant safety?

Leaks from piping are a main cause of fires and toxic releases. Correct materials, wall thickness, flexibility, supports, bolting, valves and isolation reduce leak risk. Good layout gives safe access, escape, and space for firefighting.

Link to this answer
What is a HAZOP and how does it affect piping?

A Hazard and Operability study reviews P&IDs node by node for deviations (more pressure, reverse flow, etc.). It often adds valves, check valves, relief, isolation or instrument changes that the piping design must then include.

Link to this answer
Why are small-bore connections a safety concern?

Small-bore branches (vents, drains, instrument tappings) are weak points: they break under vibration or impact. Keep them short, brace them where vibration exists, use robust connections (e.g. sockolets with a bracket) and avoid long cantilevered valves.

Link to this answer
What isolation is needed for maintenance?

Positive isolation (spectacle blinds, spades, removable spools) for confined entry and hazardous systems; double block and bleed for other hazardous work; single valve only for low-risk utilities. The client's isolation philosophy decides, and the layout must give access to fit blinds.

Link to this answer
Why must escape routes stay clear of piping?

Operators must leave a fire or release area quickly. Piping, valves and supports in walkways, low headroom or blocked ladder landings slow escape. Model reviews check escape routes and headroom.

Link to this answer
How are relief and flare systems designed safely?

Relief lines must not have pockets where liquid collects, must enter headers from the top, and need supports for reaction forces. Flare headers slope to knock-out drums. Isolation valves in relief paths are locked open and controlled by procedure.

Link to this answer
What is fire-proofing and fire-safe design?

Structures carrying important piping near fire sources get fire-proofing up to a height set by the standard. Valves in critical service may be fire-tested (API 607). Emergency isolation valves are placed outside fire zones and their actuators protected.

Link to this answer
Why are flanges minimised in hazardous service?

Every flange is a possible leak point. In toxic, sour or hydrogen service, welded construction is preferred and flanges are used only where removal or isolation needs them. Flanged joints that remain are tagged and assembled under controlled procedures.

Link to this answer
What safety issues arise during hydrotest and commissioning?

Overpressure of the wrong section, forgotten blinds, unsupported lines filled with water, trapped air, and stored energy in pneumatic tests. Test procedures, exclusion zones, calibrated gauges and relief protection on the test pump manage these risks.

Link to this answer
What is Management of Change (MOC)?

A formal process for reviewing changes to design, process or equipment so that hazards are assessed before the change is made. Field changes to piping (routing, materials, supports, valves) on a running plant go through MOC.
