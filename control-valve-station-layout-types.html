Control valve station layout types: drawings and limitations

The same block valves, drains and bypass can be arranged in several ways. Each arrangement suits some services and plots and causes problems in others.

Every station has the same parts: block valves, drains, often reducers and a globe valve bypass. What changes is how they are arranged around the plant. The P&ID tells you which valves you need; the layout decides whether operators can reach them and maintenance can take the valve out. For the parts themselves, see the control valve station guide.

1. Bypass beside the control valve, in plan

Use it for: most stations at grade or on a platform. It is the usual first choice.

Limitations:

  • Needs plot width: two lines side by side, with room for flanges and handwheels between them.
  • Put the control valve on the walkway side. A bypass between the walkway and the control valve blocks access to the positioner.
  • Support the main line and the bypass separately, so neither sags when the control valve is out.

2. Bypass above the control valve

Use it for: stations where plot width is tight, and gas or vapour lines.

Limitations:

  • The bypass must clear the actuator plus the space to lift it off. On large valves this pushes the bypass globe valve out of reach from grade.
  • The bypass becomes a high point, so liquid lines may need a vent on it.
  • Nothing else can run above the station, because that space is reserved for lifting.

3. Bypass below the control valve

Use it for: only where there is no headroom above the station, for example under a platform.

Limitations:

  • The idle bypass is a pocket. Liquid, dirt and condensate settle in it, so it needs its own low-point drain.
  • Not for services that must not pocket: slurries, viscous or freezing liquids, molten sulphur, wet gas or flare lines.
  • The bypass globe valve ends up close to grade. Check it against the minimum operating height in your project specification.

4. Station on a vertical line

Use it for: risers next to equipment when there is no horizontal run. Flow upward is preferred, so the valve stays full.

Limitations:

  • The stem is horizontal. Many valves accept this, but heavy actuators load the stem and packing on one side. Confirm with the supplier and support the actuator if needed.
  • The valves sit at different heights, so the upper block valve and the positioner often need a platform.
  • Taking the valve out means lifting it vertically out of the run. Plan a lifting point.

5. Station dropped to grade from the pipe rack

Use it for: rack lines whose control valve must be reached from grade.

Limitations:

  • The drop creates a low point that needs a drain and two high points that need vents.
  • Wet gas leaves condensate in the station; two-phase and flashing flow forms slugs in the vertical legs. Avoid it for those services.
  • Not for services that must not pocket (see layout 3).
  • The extra elbows add pressure drop, and the legs need supports, guides and, on hot lines, a stress check.

6. Angle control valve

Use it for: high pressure-drop letdown, flashing, erosive and slurry service, often discharging straight into a vessel.

Limitations:

  • The outlet must enlarge right at the valve and run straight. An elbow close to the outlet erodes quickly in flashing flow.
  • The body and actuator are heavy and hang off the outlet, so the valve needs its own support.
  • A normal bypass is awkward to fit. Many have none; if the P&ID shows one, it is routed separately.
  • Removal needs vertical lift, because the outlet flange faces down.

7. Three-way control valve

Use it for: temperature control by diverting or mixing flow around an exchanger.

Limitations:

  • Diverting and mixing bodies are different. Check the flow arrow on each port against the P&ID.
  • Each port needs a block valve, and a simple globe valve bypass does not replace the valve, so check what the P&ID asks for.
  • Hot and cold streams meet where the bypass rejoins. Ask stress to check that tee and the valve nozzles.
  • Three flanged ports need more space to spread the flanges and lift the valve out.

8. No bypass, or no block valves

Use it for: shutdown and on-off valves, where a bypass would defeat the protection; spared control valves; clean, non-critical services where the unit can stop.

Limitations:

  • Maintenance needs the unit, or that section, shut down.
  • Without block valves, isolation relies on valves further away, so more of the line has to be drained.
  • The valve still needs drains or vents to empty it before removal.

9. Self-acting regulator with a sensing line

Use it for: simple pressure control with no air or power: fuel gas, nitrogen blanketing, utility water and steam reduction.

Limitations:

  • The sensing line must tap a straight length downstream at the distance the supplier gives. Near an elbow or reducer the regulator hunts.
  • Slope the sensing line so liquid drains back to the main line, and give it an isolation valve.
  • Accuracy and capacity are limited. Large flows or tight control need a control valve.

Limits that apply to every layout

  • Reach: handwheels, the positioner and drains within operating height from grade or a platform. See valve access.
  • Lifting: clear space above the actuator to lift it off, and nothing routed through that space.
  • Removal: room to pull the flange bolts and spread the flanges, with supports on both sides so the line holds its position when the valve is out.
  • Straight runs: keep elbows and branches away from the outlet of high pressure-drop valves. See straight runs.
  • Flow direction: check the body arrow against the P&ID on every valve.
  • Hazardous fluids: drains and vents go to a closed system, not to grade.

Common questions

Which control valve station layout is most common?

The bypass beside the control valve, at the same height, at grade or on a platform. Every valve is at one height and nothing sits above the actuator.

Why avoid a bypass below the control valve?

The idle bypass becomes a pocket where liquid, dirt and condensate collect. Use it only when there is no headroom, and give it a low-point drain.

Can a control valve be installed on a vertical line?

Yes, usually with flow upward. The stem is then horizontal, so check with the supplier and support heavy actuators.

When is an angle control valve used?

For high pressure-drop letdown, flashing, erosive and slurry service. The outlet enlarges right at the valve and runs straight, often into a vessel.

Why do shutdown valves have no bypass?

A bypass would let someone route flow around the valve and defeat the protection it provides.
